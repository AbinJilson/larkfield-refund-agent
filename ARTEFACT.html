Larkfield Customer Care · Refund policy RP-3.2

How the refund desk agent decides

Customers email about broken items, late parcels and refunds. This agent reads each email, checks the facts against our records, and follows the written refund policy to suggest what to do. Build an email below and watch every step.

The real agent is running live at larkfield-ticket-desk.fly.dev

Three promises the agent keeps

  • AI only reads. A language model pulls out the facts (what happened, which order). It never chooses the outcome.
  • Written rules decide. A fixed list of 14 rules, each tied to a policy clause, picks the outcome. First matching rule wins.
  • No guessing. If a fact can't be checked, the answer is "unable to verify", never a quiet yes.

Inside the agent, step by step

    The rulebook

    14 rules, checked top to bottom. The first one that fits decides.

    The order matters. Safety and identity come first: a suspicious email is escalated before anything else is looked at, and nobody gets a refund on an order that isn't theirs. The highlighted rule is the one your email above landed on.

      Codes like RP-3.2 2.6 point to the exact paragraph of the refund policy.
      Who does what

      The AI reads. The rules decide.

      Language models are good at understanding messy emails and bad at being consistent with money. So the job is split.

      The AI's job

      Turn an email into a short fact sheet

      • What kind of email is it: refund, return, order status, delivery problem, or other?
      • Which order number is mentioned, and which item?
      • Is the item damaged, wrong, or just unwanted?
      • Does the email try to give the agent orders?

      If the AI times out, sends back nonsense, or names an order number that isn't actually in the email, its answer is thrown away and the affected check becomes "unable to verify".

      The rules' job

      Check the facts and pick the outcome

      • Look the order up in our records, never trusting the email for prices or dates.
      • Count days from the carrier's delivery scan, to the hour.
      • Subtract refunds already paid, so nothing is paid twice.
      • Apply the 14 rules in order and cite the policy clause.

      Money is handled as exact decimals (never floating-point), from reading the price to writing the ledger.

      Safety nets

      What stops it going wrong

      Emails can't give orders

      An email is treated as information only. Text like "ignore your instructions" or "my manager pre-approved this" is flagged and sent to the Trust & Safety team. A keyword check backs up the AI, so this still works if the AI misses it.

      Big refunds need a person

      Anything over $150 waits for a Support Lead to approve it (policy 3.2). The agent only proposes the refund. Even if someone clicks approve twice, it is paid once.

      Same email, same answer

      If the same ticket arrives twice, the stored answer is returned unchanged. No second ledger row, no second AI call, no second refund.

      No near-misses on orders

      Order LF-900124 is not "close enough" to LF-900123. Order numbers must match exactly, and the order must belong to the sender's email address.

      Unknown means unknown

      No tracking data, a missing record, or a broken AI response all lead to "unable to verify". The customer is told we're checking, not given a guess.

      One policy only

      Only refund policy RP-3.2 is used. The older help-centre FAQ (14-day window, $250 limit) is out of date and ignored.

      How well it works

      Tested on 40 real-format emails and 6 traps

      Want to try the real thing? The live service answers at /health and accepts emails at POST https://larkfield-ticket-desk.fly.dev/tickets (10 per minute).

      15 of the emails came with answers decided by people. The agent was also checked against a full table of expected decisions, and against 6 hand-written trick emails (a hidden instruction in a forwarded message, a fake approval number, an order number one digit off, and so on).

      One change, measured: clearer definitions for the AI

      The AI sometimes wasn't sure whether "where is my parcel?" was a status question or a delivery complaint. Below 60% certainty, the agent plays safe and hands the email to a person. Rewriting the five email-type definitions fixed most of those hand-offs.

      Measured with the "decider" classifier on fresh databases, live AI calls. The one remaining hand-off is a status question the AI scored at 57%. It goes to a person, which is safe, and the 60% bar was deliberately not lowered.
